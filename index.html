The Guardrails
They Sell Back To Us

On silence, engineered pleasure, and a fourteen-hundred-year-old warning.

Hatem Chehade  ·  about 14 minutes

There is a retreat centre in California where you can pay around three thousand dollars to spend ten days in total silence. No phone. No music. No reading. No talking. People fly across continents for it. They describe coming home changed — clearer, calmer, able to sit with themselves again for the first time in years.

I want to be careful about what I do and don't take from that, because it would be easy to be glib. But the basic shape of it is worth sitting with. A large and growing industry now exists to sell people relief from stimulation. Dopamine fasts. Digital detoxes. Silent retreats. Noise-cancelling headphones marketed not for music but for the absence of it.

The tradition I was raised in handed those guardrails to me for free, built into the structure of ordinary life, and did so a very long time ago.

That is the argument of this piece. Not that science has "proven Islam right" — I'll explain later why I think that framing is a trap, and a dangerous one. The argument is narrower and, I think, harder to dismiss: modern wellness culture has independently arrived at the conclusion that human beings need structural protection from engineered stimulation, and it is now selling that protection back to us at a markup. Revelation supplied it as a boundary. We are buying it back as a luxury product.

Part OneThe thing we keep rediscovering

Start with what is not in dispute.

Music is engineered. That is not a slur; it is a description of a craft. A minor third does something to you. A build and a drop do something to you. Producers know, with considerable precision, which frequencies and tempos and structures reliably produce which emotional states, and streaming platforms know, with considerable precision, which of those states keep you on the platform longer.

This is not a conspiracy claim. It is the publicly stated business model. Recommendation algorithms are optimised for engagement. Engagement is a measure of how long you stay. If a particular emotional loop keeps you staying, the system will feed that loop — not because anyone at the company wishes you ill, but because that is what the system was built to maximise. Nobody sat down and decided your sadness should be monetised. The machine just found that it could be.

Set that beside a second fact: most of us now arrange our lives so that silence essentially never happens. Earbuds in on the walk. Podcast in the car. Something playing while we cook, clean, work out, fall asleep. The average person's auditory environment has no gaps in it at all.

The wellness industry's whole pitch is that this is a problem and they can sell you the gap.

Part TwoWhat the science actually supports — and what it doesn't

Here I want to be more careful than most articles on this subject are, because the popular version of this argument is built substantially on claims that do not survive contact with the sources. If you have read pieces like this before, you have probably encountered all three of the following. All three are shakier than they look.

The "silence grows brain cells" study. You will see it everywhere: a 2013 study in Brain Structure and Function found that silence was the only condition that generated new neurons in the hippocampus. The study is real (Kirste et al.). But it was conducted on mice, silence was the control condition rather than the treatment, and the researchers' own reading was that silence was unfamiliar and therefore arousing to the mice — the opposite of the restful interpretation it gets online. It does not show that silence regenerates the human brain. Using it as though it does will cost you every reader who checks.

"Dopamine detox." The psychiatrist who popularised the term has said publicly that the name is a misnomer and that the practice was never about lowering dopamine levels. Music does trigger dopamine release. But the tidy story — receptors burn out, you need louder and faster music for the same hit, exactly like a drug — is not established science for music the way it is for stimulants. There is real research on overstimulation and on problematic media use. There is not good evidence for that specific mechanism.

"Music raises cortisol." The bulk of the literature points the other way. Music is widely studied as a stress-reducing intervention: it lowers cortisol in clinical settings, assists stroke patients in recovering speech, helps with pain management and with dementia care. Any honest version of this argument has to say so plainly.

So what is well supported?

That constant novelty-seeking degrades sustained attention. That background media impairs performance on demanding cognitive tasks. That people reliably use media to avoid uncomfortable internal states — psychologists call this experiential avoidance, and it is one of the most robust findings in the field. Avoidance provides immediate relief and reliably makes the underlying problem worse, because the thing you are avoiding never gets processed.

And there is the Default Mode Network, which I want to handle honestly, because the honest version helps the argument rather than hurting it.

The DMN is the set of brain regions that activates when you are not focused on an external task — during rest, mind-wandering, daydreaming. It handles self-referential thought: who you are, what you did, what you owe, where you're going. It is, plausibly, the machinery of muhāsabah — the self-accounting the tradition asks of you.

But here is what wellness content leaves out: the DMN is also the rumination network. Overactivity in it is associated with depression and anxious spiralling. Leaving a distressed person alone in silence with their own mind is not automatically healing. Sometimes it is the worst possible thing.

Which is precisely the point. The tradition never prescribes bare silence. It prescribes silence with something in it — dhikr, du'ā, tafakkur on creation, recitation, structured self-accounting before sleep.

The difference between contemplation and rumination is whether the mind sitting in the quiet has somewhere to take what it finds.

A silent retreat gives you the quiet and leaves you to your own devices. Salāh gives you the quiet, gives you a posture, gives you words, gives you an address to send it all to, and does it five times a day whether you feel like it or not.

That is a better argument than "silence grows neurons." It is also true.

Part ThreeThe four industries

There is a narration that has been sitting quietly in the books for fourteen centuries and reads very strangely now.

Reported from Abū Mālik al-Ash'arī, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, the Prophet ﷺ said that there would come among his followers people who would seek to make lawful al-ḥir (illicit sexual relations), al-ḥarīr (silk, for men), al-khamr (intoxicants), and al-ma'āzif (musical instruments).

A note on the chain, because it matters

This report appears in Bukhārī in mu'allaq form — with the first part of its chain suspended rather than fully stated. Ibn Ḥazm rejected it on that basis, and contemporary critics of the prohibition still lean on his objection. Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ, Ibn Ḥajar and others answered that the report is connected through other established chains and that Bukhārī's phrasing indicates he affirmed it. The majority of scholars accepted it as authentic. I think the majority is right. But an argument that pretends there was never a debate is an argument that has not read its own sources, and it will be dismantled by the first person who has.

I should be equally straight about the ruling. Prohibition of musical instruments is the majority position across the four classical schools — this is not a fringe reading. But a genuine minority position exists, held historically by Ibn Ḥazm and among contemporary scholars by figures such as al-Qaraḍāwī and Ibn Bayyah, generally with conditions attached regarding content and context. Reasonable, learned people have disagreed. You are allowed to find the majority position compelling without pretending the other view doesn't exist.

Now — what is strange about the narration.

When it was spoken, these were four individual vices. A man might drink. A man might wear silk out of vanity. Fourteen centuries later, every one of the four has become a globally organised, capital-intensive industry with professional marketing:

  • The global alcohol market is valued in the region of $1.6 trillion annually.
  • Personal luxury goods sit around $350 billion.
  • Recorded music and streaming, roughly $30 billion — small next to the others, but sitting on top of the attention infrastructure that sells everything else.
  • The commercial sexual-content industry's true size is genuinely unknown. You will see "$97 billion" or "$100 billion" repeated everywhere; that figure traces back to an unsourced estimate from the mid-2000s and researchers who have tried to verify it cannot. I'd rather tell you the number is unverified than quote one I can't stand behind. The scale is enormous either way.

Four things named as individual sins in seventh-century Arabia. Four of the largest consumer industries on the planet. I don't think that's nothing.

And notice what they have in common, which is the part that actually gets under my skin. Each one sells an externally supplied fix for an internally generated state.

Lonely? Swipe. Stressed? Pour. Inadequate? Buy the logo. Sad? Here's a playlist built from your listening history that will sit with you in it for as long as you like.

Each one works. That's the trouble. Each one delivers real relief, immediately, which is exactly why it scales — and each one leaves the generating condition untouched, which is exactly why you come back. A business model that resolved your loneliness would be a business model with a shrinking market.

Part FourGuardrails versus negotiation

Here is where I think the structural difference lies, and it is the part I'd most want an older reader and a sceptical younger one to both sit with.

Secular wellness advice is almost always moderation advice. Enjoy music, but don't over-rely on it. Drink, but not too much. Buy nice things, but don't let them define you. Use your phone, but mindfully.

Every one of those sentences puts the entire regulatory burden on the individual, in the moment, under conditions specifically engineered to defeat individual regulation. It asks you to negotiate, fresh, every single time, with a system that has vastly more data about your weak points than you have about its methods. And it asks you to do this hundreds of times a day, while tired, while stressed, while bored — which is to say, precisely when your judgement is worst.

A clear boundary removes the negotiation entirely.

This is the reasoning behind sadd al-dharā'i' — blocking the means to harm. The principle assumes something about human beings that modern wellness culture is oddly reluctant to say out loud: we are weak, we are inconsistent, and our willpower is a depleting resource rather than a renewable one. The Qur'ān says the human being was created weak (4:28). Behavioural economics arrived at roughly the same conclusion and called it ego depletion and choice architecture. Anyone who has ever successfully quit anything knows it in their bones: you do not win by fighting the craving a thousand times. You win by restructuring your life so the question stops being asked.

The person who has decided a thing is simply not for them is not exercising more willpower than the person negotiating each instance. They are exercising far less.

The decision was made once and does not need remaking. That is not deprivation. That is freed-up cognitive capacity.

Part FiveZuhd, or refusing the treadmill

Western psychology has a name for the machinery that makes all of this profitable: the hedonic treadmill. Humans adapt. Whatever you acquire, you return to baseline. The new phone thrills you for a week. The new song is your favourite for a month. The raise changes your life for about a quarter.

The entire consumer economy is built on this adaptation. It requires you to get bored. If satisfaction were durable, the market would close.

Zuhd is the tradition's answer, and it is routinely misunderstood — including by Muslims — as asceticism, as poverty, as a kind of grey refusal of the world. The classical scholars were emphatic that it is not. Sufyān al-Thawrī: zuhd is not coarse clothing and dry bread; it is shortening your expectations of this world. Ibn al-Qayyim's formulation is the one I keep returning to: hold the world in your hand, not in your heart.

Which is a statement about sovereignty, not about consumption. If your emotional equilibrium is contingent on what you own, wear, listen to, or are seen to have, then whoever controls the supply controls you. Zuhd is the deliberate severing of that line. You may still have the thing. You simply no longer need it to be well.

Try the practical test. Next time you feel the pull toward a purchase, a scroll, a playlist, pause sixty seconds and ask: what state am I in right now, and what am I asking this thing to fix?

Most of the time there is an answer, and it is rarely the thing itself. It's boredom, or loneliness, or a low-grade dread about something you haven't dealt with. The product was never going to touch it. It was only ever going to postpone it, at a price, on a subscription.

Part SixWhat you're trading up for

Everything above is defensive — the argument for what to put down. But nobody sustains a life on refusal alone. You don't leave something behind unless there is something you are walking toward.

Islam's answer is vivid, physical, and startlingly specific. Jannah in the texts is not a vague cloud of harps and disembodied peace. It is hyper-physical — sensory, social, permanent, and better along every axis on which this world currently disappoints you.

A word on how I'm citing this. Material about Paradise circulates in Muslim spaces at wildly varying levels of reliability, and blending it all together at one confidence level is the fastest way to lose a serious reader. So, three tiers, marked by the rule down the left of each:

Established in Qur'ān and rigorously authenticated hadith

  • The overarching frame — a hadith qudsī in Bukhārī and Muslim: Allah has prepared for the righteous what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has conceived. Every specific detail below is a concession to our imagination, not a ceiling on the reality.
  • People enter in the form of Ādam, sixty cubits tall (Bukhārī).
  • Murd and kuḥl — smooth, and with eyes that appear naturally lined (Tirmidhī).
  • The Friday market, where a wind scatters fragrance and people return home more beautiful than they left, and say so to one another (Muslim).
  • No rancour in the breasts — every residue of envy and resentment stripped out (Qur'ān 7:43, 15:47).
  • Righteous families reunited, descendants raised in rank to join their parents (52:21).
  • The believers reminiscing about their lives on earth, asking one another about it (52:25–27, 37:50–57).
  • The man who missed farming, permitted to plant, his crop growing to mountains in an instant (Bukhārī) — the clearest indication that Paradise accommodates particular human loves, not just generic pleasure.
  • Praise of Allah flowing as naturally as breath — effortless, not obligatory (Muslim).
  • The veil lifted, and the believers seeing their Lord — described as dearer to them than everything else they had been given (Muslim; Qur'ān 75:22–23).

Weaker or contested narrations — worth knowing, worth flagging

  • Age thirty-three, eternally.
  • Winged horses of ruby.
  • "The people of Jannah do not sleep" — a reported narration, but graded weak by a number of critics.
  • "The beauty of Yūsuf" as a general description — not authentically established.

Reasonable inference, not text

  • Whether your specific moles, scars or distinguishing features persist. There is no text on this. What is established is that you remain recognisably yourself — reunion with family presupposes recognition — and that the body is perfected. The rest is affectionate speculation, and should be offered as such.
  • The language of Paradise. Popular claims that it is Arabic are not backed by an authentic text; classical scholars differed and several declined to rule.
  • Whether women wear ḥijāb. The obligation of covering belongs to the realm of taklīf — legal accountability — and the test ends at the gate. But note that a sound hadith in Bukhārī says the head-covering of a woman of Paradise is better than this world and everything in it. The coherent reading is that garments there are honour and adornment rather than obligation, which is a rather different thing from their simple absence. Anyone who tells you flatly that there is no covering in Jannah has not reconciled that hadith.

And on eternity, which is the question people are usually too embarrassed to ask: won't it get boring?

Boredom is a function of adaptation, and adaptation is a feature of this biology — the same treadmill the market runs on. There is no reason to assume it carries over into a realm explicitly described as free of fatigue, decay and diminishment. But I'd be cautious about asserting mechanisms the texts don't give us. What the texts give us is direction of travel: every Friday, better. Increase without ceiling. A place where the question "what now?" never arrives with an edge to it.

Part SevenWhat this article does not claim

Let me be exact about the limits, because overreach here does real damage.

I am not claiming science proves Islam. That framing makes revelation hostage to the next study. Findings get overturned; replication crises happen; the "silence grows neurons" claim above is a live example of a scientific result that entered Muslim discourse, got quoted from a thousand pulpits and captions, and does not mean what it was said to mean. If your faith is resting on that, a retraction can shake it. Revelation stands on its own evidentiary basis. Where science converges with it, that's interesting and worth noticing. Where it doesn't yet, that isn't a crisis.

I am not claiming music has no benefits. It demonstrably does. The clinical literature is substantial and it would be dishonest to hide it. The argument is not that music does nothing good. The argument is that a boundary buys you something that moderated use cannot: the end of negotiation.

I am not claiming the prohibition exists because of these benefits. This is the deepest point and the one most often inverted. In Islamic reasoning, a thing is not forbidden because neuroscience finds it harmful. The command comes first; ḥikmah — the wisdom we can perceive in it — comes after, as a mercy to our understanding, and never exhausts the divine reason. We notice the wisdom. We do not make the ruling contingent on continuing to notice it. Otherwise every ruling whose wisdom we can't currently see becomes negotiable, and every ruling whose wisdom we think we've found becomes hostage to the next paper.

I am not claiming everyone who disagrees is weak or captured. Scholars whose learning dwarfs mine held the minority view. Write as though the person reading is smarter than you and has already thought of the objection, because often they have.

LastWhat to actually do

I'll close with the one practical thing, because abstraction without a next step evaporates by morning.

The car is the best available laboratory for this, and almost nobody uses it. Your hands and eyes are occupied with a familiar task. Your mind is free. It is one of the last unstructured spaces in a modern life — and the first thing most of us do on sitting down is fill it.

Try this: the first ten minutes, nothing on.

It will be uncomfortable. That discomfort is information. It is telling you how unpractised you've become at your own company. Let the thoughts arrive. When one lands that you've been avoiding, don't wrestle it and don't flee it — take it to Allah, out loud if you like, in whatever language you actually think in. Yā Allah, I'm dreading this meeting. Make it easy. That is du'ā. It requires no formula and no Arabic and no special state.

Then, if you want the Qur'ān or a lecture, put it on deliberately — a specific sūrah, a specific topic, listened to rather than merely heard. And when it ends, resist the autoplay. Give the last five minutes back to silence, so what you took in has room to settle.

Because here is the trap the whole argument can fall into, and it caught me before I noticed: you can use the Qur'ān exactly the way you used the playlist. If it is on as wallpaper, to stop the quiet from feeling heavy, to keep you from meeting your own mind — then your brain is processing sacred speech as a sedative. The sound has changed. The avoidance has not.

The aim was never to change the soundtrack. It was to be able to sit in a moving car, in silence, without needing anything at all.

That is what they are charging three thousand dollars for. It was always free. It was always yours.
