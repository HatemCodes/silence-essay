The Guardrails
They Sell Back To Us

On silence, engineered pleasure, and a fourteen-hundred-year-old warning.

Hatem Chehade  ·  about 14 minutes

There is a retreat centre in California where you can pay around three thousand dollars to spend ten days in total silence. No phone. No music. No reading. No talking. People fly across continents for it. They describe coming home changed, clearer, calmer, able to sit with themselves again for the first time in years.

I want to be careful about what I do and don't take from that, because it would be easy to make a cheap point out of it. But the basic shape of it is worth sitting with. A large and growing industry now exists to sell people relief from stimulation. Dopamine fasts. Digital detoxes. Silent retreats. Noise-cancelling headphones marketed not for music but for the absence of it.

The tradition I was raised in handed those guardrails to me for free, built into the structure of ordinary life, and did so a very long time ago.

That is the argument of this piece. Not that science has "proven Islam right", I'll explain later why I think that framing is a trap, and a dangerous one. The argument is narrower and, I think, harder to dismiss: modern wellness culture has independently arrived at the conclusion that human beings need structural protection from engineered stimulation, and it is now selling that protection back to us at a markup. Revelation supplied it as a boundary. We are buying it back as a luxury product.

Part OneThe thing we keep rediscovering

Start with what is not in dispute.

Music is engineered. That is not an insult; it is a description of a craft. A minor chord does something to you. A build and a drop do something to you. Producers know, with considerable precision, which sounds and tempos and structures reliably produce which emotional states, and streaming platforms know, with considerable precision, which of those states keep you on the platform longer.

This is not a conspiracy claim. It is the publicly stated business model. Recommendation algorithms are built to maximise engagement, and engagement is simply a measure of how long you stay. If a particular emotional loop keeps you staying, the system will feed that loop, not because anyone at the company necessarily wishes you ill, but because that is what the system was built to do. Nobody sat down and decided your sadness should be sold. The machine just found that it could be.

Set that beside a second fact: most of us now arrange our lives so that silence essentially never happens. Earbuds in on the walk. Podcast in the car. Something playing while we cook, clean, work out, fall asleep. The average person's day has no gaps of quiet in it at all.

The wellness industry's whole pitch is that this is a problem and they can sell you the gap.

Part TwoWhat the science actually supports, and what it doesn't

Here I want to be more careful than most articles on this subject are, because the popular version of this argument is built substantially on claims that fall apart the moment you check the sources. If you have read pieces like this before, you have probably met all three of the following. All three are shakier than they look.

The "silence grows brain cells" study. You will see it everywhere: a 2013 study in Brain Structure and Function found that silence was the only condition that grew new brain cells in the hippocampus, the part of the brain that handles memory and learning. The study is real (Kirste et al.). But it was done on mice, silence was the control condition rather than the thing being tested, and the researchers' own reading was that silence was unfamiliar to the mice and therefore stimulating, the opposite of the restful meaning it gets online. It does not show that silence rebuilds the human brain. Using it as though it does will cost you every reader who checks.

"Dopamine detox." The psychiatrist who made the term popular has said publicly that the name is misleading and that the practice was never about lowering dopamine levels. Music does trigger dopamine release. But the tidy story, that your receptors burn out and you need louder and faster music for the same hit, exactly like a drug, is not established science for music the way it is for stimulants. There is real research on overstimulation and on problem media use. There is not good evidence for that specific mechanism.

"Music raises cortisol." The bulk of the research points the other way. Music is widely studied as a stress-reducing treatment: it lowers cortisol in clinical settings, helps stroke patients recover speech, and assists with pain management and dementia care. Any honest version of this argument has to say so plainly.

So what is well supported?

That constantly chasing something new wears down your ability to hold your attention on one thing. That having something playing in the background makes you worse at tasks which need real thought. And that people reliably use media to avoid uncomfortable feelings, which psychologists call experiential avoidance, and which is one of the most solid findings in the field. Avoidance gives immediate relief and reliably makes the underlying problem worse, because the thing you are avoiding never gets dealt with.

And there is the Default Mode Network, which I want to handle honestly, because the honest version helps the argument rather than hurting it.

The DMN is the set of brain regions that switches on when you are not focused on something outside you: during rest, mind-wandering, daydreaming. It handles thinking about yourself. Who you are, what you did, what you owe, where you're going. It is, plausibly, the machinery of muhāsabah, the honest self-accounting the tradition asks of you.

But here is what wellness content leaves out: the DMN is also the network behind rumination, the loop of turning the same worry over and over. Too much activity in it is linked to depression and anxious spirals. Leaving a distressed person alone in silence with their own mind is not automatically healing. Sometimes it is the worst possible thing.

Which is precisely the point. The tradition never prescribes bare silence. It prescribes silence with something in it: dhikr, du'ā, tafakkur on creation, recitation, an honest look at your day before sleep.

The difference between reflection and rumination is whether the mind sitting in the quiet has somewhere to take what it finds.

A silent retreat gives you the quiet and leaves you to your own devices. Salāh gives you the quiet, gives you a posture, gives you words, gives you an address to send it all to, and does it five times a day whether you feel like it or not.

That is a better argument than "silence grows neurons." It is also true.

Part ThreeThe four industries

There is a narration that has been sitting quietly in the books for fourteen centuries and reads very strangely now.

Reported from Abū Mālik al-Ash'arī, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, the Prophet ﷺ said that there would come among his followers people who would seek to make lawful al-ḥir (illicit sexual relations), al-ḥarīr (silk, for men), al-khamr (intoxicants), and al-ma'āzif (musical instruments).

A note on the chain, because it matters and because someone will raise it

This report appears in Bukhārī in mu'allaq form, meaning the first narrators in its chain are not named. Ibn Ḥazm rejected it on that basis, and critics of the prohibition today still lean on his objection. Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ, Ibn Ḥajar and others answered that the report is connected through other established chains, and that the way Bukhārī phrases it shows he accepted it. The majority of scholars accepted it as authentic. I think the majority is right. But an argument that pretends there was never a debate is an argument that has not read its own sources, and it will be taken apart by the first person who has.

I should be equally straight about the ruling. Prohibition of musical instruments is the majority position across all four classical schools. This is not a fringe reading. But a genuine minority position exists, held historically by Ibn Ḥazm and among scholars today by figures such as al-Qaraḍāwī and Ibn Bayyah, usually with conditions attached about content and context. Reasonable, learned people have disagreed. You are allowed to find the majority position convincing without pretending the other view doesn't exist.

Now, what is strange about the narration.

When it was spoken, these were four individual vices. A man might drink. A man might wear silk out of vanity. Fourteen centuries later, every one of the four has become a globally organised, heavily funded industry with a professional marketing department:

  • The global alcohol market is valued in the region of $1.6 trillion a year.
  • Personal luxury goods sit around $350 billion.
  • Recorded music and streaming, roughly $30 billion. Small next to the others, but sitting on top of the attention machinery that sells everything else.
  • The commercial sexual-content industry's true size is genuinely unknown. You will see "$97 billion" or "$100 billion" repeated everywhere; that figure traces back to an unsourced estimate from the mid-2000s, and researchers who have tried to verify it cannot. I'd rather tell you the number is unverified than quote one I can't stand behind. The scale is enormous either way, and most people know it first-hand from friends or family.

Four things named as individual sins in seventh-century Arabia. Four of the largest consumer industries on the planet. I don't think that's nothing.

And notice what they have in common, which is the part that actually gets under my skin. Each one sells you an outside fix for a feeling that started inside you.

Lonely? Swipe. Stressed? Pour. Not enough? Buy the logo. Sad? Here's a playlist built from your listening history that will sit with you in it for as long as you like.

Each one works. That's the trouble. Each one delivers real relief, immediately, which is exactly why it grows, and each one leaves the thing causing the feeling untouched, which is exactly why you come back. A business that cured your loneliness would be a business with a shrinking market.

Part FourGuardrails versus negotiation

Here is where I think the real difference lies, and it is the part I'd most want an older reader and a sceptical younger one to both sit with.

Secular wellness advice is almost always moderation advice. Enjoy music, but don't over-rely on it. Drink, but not too much. Buy nice things, but don't let them define you. Use your phone, but mindfully.

Every one of those sentences puts the entire job of self-control on you, in the moment, under conditions specifically designed to defeat self-control. It asks you to negotiate, fresh, every single time, with a system that knows far more about your weak points than you know about its methods. And it asks you to do this hundreds of times a day, while tired, while stressed, while bored, which is to say, precisely when your judgement is at its worst.

A clear boundary removes the negotiation entirely.

This is the reasoning behind sadd al-dharā'i', blocking the roads that lead to harm. The principle assumes something about human beings that modern wellness culture is oddly unwilling to say out loud: we are weak, we are inconsistent, and willpower runs out rather than renewing itself. The Qur'ān says the human being was created weak (4:28). Behavioural economics reached roughly the same conclusion and gave it names like ego depletion and choice architecture. Anyone who has ever successfully quit anything knows it in their bones: you do not win by fighting the craving a thousand times. You win by rebuilding your life so the question stops being asked.

The person who has decided a thing is simply not for them is not using more willpower than the person negotiating each time. They are using far less.

The decision was made once and does not need remaking. That is not deprivation. That is mental energy handed back to you.

Part FiveZuhd, or refusing the treadmill

Western psychology has a name for the machinery that makes all of this profitable: the hedonic treadmill. Humans adapt. Whatever you get, you drift back to where you started. The new phone thrills you for a week. The new song is your favourite for a month. The raise changes your life for about three months.

The entire consumer economy is built on this drift. It needs you to get bored. If satisfaction lasted, the market would close.

Zuhd is the tradition's answer, and it is routinely misunderstood, including by Muslims, as self-denial, as poverty, as a kind of grey refusal of the world. The classical scholars were emphatic that it is not. Sufyān al-Thawrī: zuhd is not coarse clothing and dry bread; it is shortening your expectations of this world. Ibn al-Qayyim's version is the one I keep returning to: hold the world in your hand, not in your heart.

Which is a statement about who owns you, not about what you own. If your emotional balance depends on what you have, wear, listen to, or are seen with, then whoever controls the supply controls you. Zuhd is the deliberate cutting of that line. You may still have the thing. You simply no longer need it in order to be well.

Try the practical test. Next time you feel the pull toward a purchase, a scroll, a playlist, pause sixty seconds and ask: what state am I in right now, and what am I asking this thing to fix?

Most of the time there is an answer, and it is rarely the thing itself. It's boredom, or loneliness, or a low-grade dread about something you haven't dealt with. The product was never going to touch it. It was only ever going to postpone it, at a price, on a subscription.

Part SixWhat you're trading up for

Everything above is defensive, the argument for what to put down. But nobody sustains a life on refusal alone. You don't leave something behind unless there is something you are walking toward.

Islam's answer is vivid, physical, and startlingly specific. Jannah (Paradise) in the texts is not a vague cloud of harps and disembodied peace. It is intensely physical: sensory, social, permanent, and better along every axis on which this world currently disappoints you.

A word on how I'm citing this. Material about Paradise circulates in Muslim spaces at wildly varying levels of reliability, and blending it all together at one level of confidence is the fastest way to lose a serious reader. So, three tiers, marked by the line down the left of each:

Established in Qur'ān and rigorously authenticated hadith

  • The frame around everything else, a hadith qudsī (a narration in which the Prophet ﷺ reports the words of Allah directly) in Bukhārī and Muslim: Allah has prepared for the righteous what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has ever imagined. Every specific detail below is given so we can picture it, not as a limit on the reality.
  • People enter in the form of Ādam, sixty cubits tall (Bukhārī).
  • Murd and kuḥl: smooth, with eyes that look naturally lined (Tirmidhī).
  • The Friday market, where a wind scatters fragrance and people return home more beautiful than they left, and say so to one another (Muslim).
  • No ill-will in the hearts: every trace of envy and resentment removed (Qur'ān 7:43, 15:47).
  • Righteous families reunited, descendants raised in rank to join their parents (52:21).
  • The believers remembering their lives on earth together, asking one another about it (52:25–27, 37:50–57).
  • The man who missed farming, allowed to plant, his crop growing to the size of mountains in an instant (Bukhārī). The clearest sign that Paradise makes room for particular human loves, not just general pleasure.
  • Praise of Allah flowing as naturally as breathing: effortless, not a duty (Muslim).
  • The veil lifted, and the believers seeing their Lord, described as dearer to them than everything else they had been given (Muslim; Qur'ān 75:22–23).

Weaker or contested narrations, worth knowing, worth flagging

  • Age thirty-three, forever.
  • Winged horses of ruby.
  • "The people of Jannah do not sleep": a reported narration, but graded weak by a number of the hadith critics.
  • "The beauty of Yūsuf" as a general description: not authentically established.

Reasonable inference, not text

  • Whether your specific moles, scars or distinguishing features stay. There is no text on this. What is established is that you remain recognisably yourself, since reunion with family assumes you can be recognised, and that the body is perfected. The rest is fond guesswork, and should be offered as such.
  • The language of Paradise. Popular claims that it is Arabic are not backed by an authentic text; classical scholars differed, and several refused to rule on it.
  • Whether women wear ḥijāb. The obligation to cover belongs to taklīf, the realm of being held accountable, and the test ends at the gate. But note that a sound hadith in Bukhārī says the head-covering of a woman of Paradise is better than this world and everything in it. The reading that makes sense of both is that garments there are honour and beauty rather than a duty, which is a rather different thing from their simple absence. Anyone who tells you flatly that there is no covering in Jannah has not reconciled that hadith.

And on eternity, which is the question people are usually too embarrassed to ask: won't it get boring?

Boredom comes from getting used to things, and getting used to things is a feature of this biology, the same treadmill the market runs on. There is no reason to assume it carries over into a place explicitly described as free of tiredness, decay and wearing out. But I'd be careful about claiming mechanisms the texts don't give us. What the texts do give us is a direction of travel: every Friday, better. Increase without a ceiling. A place where the question "what now?" never arrives with an edge to it.

Part SevenWhat this article does not claim

Let me be exact about the limits, because claiming too much here does real damage.

I am not claiming science proves Islam. That framing makes revelation depend on the next study. Findings get overturned; results fail when other researchers try to repeat them; the "silence grows neurons" claim above is a live example of a scientific result that entered Muslim discourse, got quoted from a thousand pulpits and captions, and does not mean what it was said to mean. If your faith is resting on that, one correction can shake it. Revelation stands on its own evidence. Where science lines up with it, that's interesting and worth noticing. Where it doesn't yet, that isn't a crisis.

I am not claiming music has no benefits. It clearly does. The clinical research is substantial and it would be dishonest to hide it. The argument is not that music does nothing good. The argument is that a boundary buys you something moderation cannot: the end of negotiation.

I am not claiming the prohibition exists because of these benefits. This is the deepest point and the one most often turned upside down. In Islamic reasoning, a thing is not forbidden because neuroscience finds it harmful. The command comes first; ḥikmah, the wisdom we can see in it, comes after, as a mercy to our understanding, and never accounts for the whole of the divine reason. We notice the wisdom. We do not make the ruling depend on continuing to notice it. Otherwise every ruling whose wisdom we can't currently see becomes negotiable, and every ruling whose wisdom we think we've found becomes hostage to the next paper.

I am not claiming everyone who disagrees is weak or captured. Scholars whose learning dwarfs mine held the minority view. Write as though the person reading is smarter than you and has already thought of the objection, because often they have.

What to actually do

I'll close with the one practical thing, because an idea without a next step evaporates by morning.

The car is the best laboratory available for this, and almost nobody uses it. Your hands and eyes are busy with a familiar task. Your mind is free. It is one of the last unfilled spaces in modern life, and the first thing most of us do when we sit down is fill it.

Try this: the first ten minutes, nothing on.

It will be uncomfortable. That discomfort is information. It is telling you how out of practice you've become at your own company. Let the thoughts arrive. When one lands that you've been avoiding, don't wrestle it and don't run from it, take it to Allah, out loud if you like, in whatever language you actually think in. Yā Allah, I'm dreading this meeting. Make it easy. That is du'ā. It requires no formula and no Arabic and no special state.

Then, if you want the Qur'ān or a lecture, put it on deliberately, a specific sūrah, a specific topic, listened to rather than merely heard. And when it ends, resist the autoplay. Give the last five minutes back to silence, so what you took in has room to settle.

Because here is the trap the whole argument can fall into, and it caught me before I noticed: you can use the Qur'ān exactly the way you used the playlist. If it is on as wallpaper, to stop the quiet from feeling heavy, to keep you from meeting your own mind, then your brain is treating sacred speech as a sedative. The sound has changed. The avoidance has not.

The aim was never to change the soundtrack. It was to be able to sit in a moving car, in silence, without needing anything at all.

That is what they are charging three thousand dollars for. It was always free. It was always yours.
